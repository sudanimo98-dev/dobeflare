FAQ ▸
What is this?
DobeFlare is a demo tool that simulates how a Trust & Safety team triages incoming incidents by severity. Instead of relying on gut feel or whoever escalates the loudest, it scores each incident using a structured model and outputs a priority tier with an SLA target.
How do I use it?
Fill in the incident form on the left — pick a threat category, set blast radius, severity, and velocity sliders, and check any escalation flags that apply. Hit Submit and it scores and queues the incident automatically. Or click "Load examples" to see pre-built scenarios across all four priority tiers.
How does the scoring work?
Each incident is scored out of 100 across five factors: threat category base weight (up to 30 pts), blast radius — how many users or systems are affected (up to 25 pts), severity (up to 20 pts), velocity — how fast it's spreading (up to 15 pts), and escalation flags like press exposure, legal involvement, or executive visibility (up to 12 pts). The total determines the priority tier: P0 ≥ 70, P1 ≥ 45, P2 ≥ 22, P3 below that.
Why those category weights?
The weights reflect real-world impact and blast radius potential. Malware (30) and Legal (28) sit highest because they spread fast and carry regulatory or infrastructure consequences. AI & Synthetic (25) and Fraud (22) follow. Content Policy violations (12) sit lowest — still important, but typically contained to specific content with slower spread. The weights aren't arbitrary; they're calibrated to match the urgency a T&S team would assign in practice.
Is this a real production tool?
No — it's a portfolio demo. Built to show how I think about incident prioritization structure, not as production software. The scoring model is based on real triage frameworks I worked with across Google and TikTok, adapted into a standalone tool.
Why did you build it?
While interviewing for incident response roles, I kept getting asked how I prioritize when everything looks equally urgent. It reminded me of a real problem at Google — multiple P1s landing simultaneously with no structured way to differentiate them beyond gut feel. DobeFlare is the tool I wish we'd had.

Incident Response Lifecycle
1
Detection
T+0:00 — 07:41 AM
Automated velocity alert fired after 340 successful logins from 4 distinct IP clusters within an 11-minute window — well outside normal user behavior thresholds. Secondary signals confirmed: payment method change rate spiked to 18% of session volume (baseline <0.3%), and Adobe Sign API recorded 60 contract executions in rapid succession across unrelated accounts. No single signal would have triggered a P0 alone; the combination of login velocity, payment mutation rate, and contract anomaly pattern elevated the alert to critical.
2
Assessment
T+0:08 — 07:49 AM
DobeFlare scored the incident at 83/100 → P0 Critical based on: Account Compromise category (20 pts), Small blast radius (5 pts), severity 9/10 for active financial harm (18 pts), velocity 8/10 for automated attack pace (12 pts), and 3 escalation flags — Legal, XFN, Active (9 pts). XFN response initiated immediately across five teams:
Engineering
  • Block 4 identified IP clusters at edge layer
  • Identify MFA re-auth gap on payment changes
  • Pull session logs for affected accounts
  • Assess broader exposure beyond 340 accounts
Legal
  • Review 60 fraudulent Adobe Sign contracts for validity
  • Assess liability exposure under Authenticity: Fraud policy
  • Determine notification obligations (GDPR, CCPA)
  • Advise on contract voiding process
Customer Trust
  • Draft victim notification templates
  • Coordinate account recovery workflows
  • Monitor support ticket volume for downstream reports
  • Manage public-facing comms if press escalation occurs
Security / InfoSec
  • Identify credential breach source (third-party dataset)
  • Cross-reference IPs against threat intel feeds
  • Assess whether attack tooling is known / commoditized
  • Determine if additional Adobe services are exposed
Policy
  • Confirm policy violation classification (Authenticity: Fraud)
  • Review account security policy for step-up auth gaps
  • Flag for post-incident policy update review
3
Containment
T+0:22 — 08:03 AM
All 340 compromised accounts suspended and flagged for forced password reset. 4 IP clusters blocked at the network edge. Adobe Sign contracts executed during the attack window placed in legal hold pending review. Payment method changes made during the attack window reversed. Session tokens for affected accounts invalidated to terminate any active attacker sessions. Rate limiting applied globally on payment method change endpoints as an interim control pending the MFA fix.
4
Investigation
T+1:15 — 08:56 AM
Credential analysis confirmed attack used a third-party breach dataset (non-Adobe source) in a classic credential stuffing pattern — no internal data breach. 340 accounts confirmed compromised; broader scan identified 12 additional accounts with suspicious payment mutations not yet flagged by the original alert. IP cluster attribution linked to a known automated fraud-as-a-service operation. Critical finding: Adobe's account security policy did not require re-authentication for payment method changes within an authenticated session — a gap that enabled the entire financial abuse chain. Without this gap, attackers would have been blocked from swapping payment info even with valid credentials.
5
Remediation & Recovery
T+4:00 — 11:41 AM
Engineering deployed step-up MFA re-authentication requirement for all payment method changes — patch applied to production within 4 hours. 352 affected accounts (340 original + 12 from expanded scan) restored via guided recovery flow with identity verification. 60 fraudulent Adobe Sign contracts voided by Legal; counterparties notified. Affected users received direct notification with explanation, recovery steps, and credit for service disruption. Threat intel on attacker IPs and tooling shared with Adobe Security's threat feed for future detection.
6
Post-Incident Follow-Up
T+48h — Post-Incident Review
Damage & Cost
352 accounts compromised; 60 fraudulent contracts executed; estimated $14K in reversed payment transactions. Engineering remediation cost: ~40 eng-hours. Customer Trust recovery effort: ~80 hours across account recovery and comms. Legal review: ~12 hours. Total estimated cost: ~$28K fully loaded.
Policy Gap
Yes — a preventable gap. Account security policy did not mandate step-up re-authentication for sensitive account mutations (payment method, email, recovery contact). Had this requirement existed, the financial abuse chain would have been blocked even after credential compromise. Policy updated to require MFA re-auth for all Tier 1 account changes.
Procedure Failure
Initial velocity alert fired but was initially routed to Tier 1 analyst queue rather than auto-escalating on payment mutation co-signal. Runbook updated to treat payment method spike + login velocity as a compound P0 trigger requiring immediate senior analyst assignment, bypassing Tier 1 triage.
Response Quality
XFN coordination was effective once escalated; Engineering patch deployed within SLA. Gap: 8-minute delay between initial alert and P0 declaration due to routing issue above. T+0:22 containment met the ≤10 min SLA from declaration, not from first signal — SLA definition to be reviewed for compound-signal incidents.
Comms Timeliness
Internal stakeholders (Engineering, Legal, Customer Trust, Policy, Security) all notified within 10 minutes of P0 declaration. Affected user notifications sent within 6 hours of containment — within GDPR 72-hour window. No press escalation occurred; proactive comms prep was not needed but was ready.
Re-infection Risk
Low. MFA step-up patch closes the primary exploitation vector. Attacker IPs blocklisted. Credential stuffing dataset identified — impacted users prompted to change passwords not just on Adobe but advised on reuse risk. Rate limiting on payment endpoints remains as a defense-in-depth layer.
Lessons Learned
Single-signal alert thresholds are insufficient for compound attack patterns. Payment mutation rate should have been a co-primary signal from day one. Step-up authentication on sensitive account mutations is table-stakes security hygiene — this gap should have been caught in a security review. Post-incident: all Tier 1 account mutations now require re-auth; compound-signal detection added to alert ruleset; runbook revised to reflect multi-signal P0 criteria.
